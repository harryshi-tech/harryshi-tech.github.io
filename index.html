About me

I'm a master's student in Mechanical Engineering at Carnegie Mellon University, working in the Biorobotics Lab with Prof. Howie Choset. I grew up curious about how animals move, and I've been asking what happens when you give a robot a body that behaves more like theirs: flexible, many-jointed, and in constant contact with its surroundings. Bodies like that are hard to design, model, and control — and they can reach places other robots can't. That's what I find interesting.

That curiosity first took concrete form at Bucknell University, where I worked on soft crawling robots inspired by caterpillars and centipedes, designing the mechanisms and gaits that make them move. A summer at MIT simulating flapping wings for a micro-aerial robot pushed me further into robot modeling and control. At CMU, my work adds another layer: a snake robot navigating the outside of a complex pipe network has to figure out how to move robustly across varying pipe geometries, while keeping track of where it is from what its own body senses. These experiences have grounded me in the full path from mechanism to control to state estimation, and made me want to keep working on robots that learn from the many ways animals move to navigate unstructured environments.

I'm hoping to continue this in a PhD, working on these questions in depth — building robots that move in ways we haven't built before, so they can operate where nothing else can.

Research / 01

Autonomous external pipe navigation for snake robots

Biorobotics Lab, Robotics Institute, Carnegie Mellon · advised by Prof. Howie Choset · 2025–present

Snake robot navigation across different geometries on the physical pipe network.

The problem

Intricate pipe networks are essential across many industries, such as chemical processing plants, power generation facilities, and municipal water distribution pipelines, but their complex geometries make inspection and maintenance difficult and unsafe for humans. Snake robots are well-suited for these environments because their hyper-redundant bodies can conform to tight, irregular pipe structures.

Our lab previously developed a set of kinematic gaits to navigate the exterior of pipes and negotiate various geometries: a rolling helix for climbing straight pipes, a windowed variant that reaches across a perpendicular T-junction, and a spiraling gait that rotates the robot around the pipe to pass obstructions.

However, the current system executes these gaits open-loop. Without contact feedback, the robot must grip conservatively to avoid slipping, which loads the joint actuators heavily and limits gait speed. The windowed rolling helix, which lets the head reach onto a new branch while the tail keeps its grip, also depends on knowing when the robot is approaching a junction and how it is oriented relative to that junction, and at present the operator supplies both by visual inspection, which fails as soon as the pipe run leaves line of sight.

The objective of this project is to close both loops onboard: to regulate contact from the robot's own torque sensing, and to infer the surrounding geometry from its own proprioception.

Snake robot climbs by wrapping around the pipe in a rolling helix. Click to enlarge.

Contributions

Compliant control: deviation-driven shape-based compliance (DSBC) controller

Standard shape-based compliance cannot distinguish the contact load from the steady load required to hold the robot on the pipe, and consequently relaxes its grip under gravitational loading alone. I developed the DSBC controller to resolve this: it filters the measured joint torque against a reference, treats only the deviation as contact, and modulates the commanded joint angles to tighten or loosen the wrap in response. Removing the need to over-grip reduced traversal times by roughly half across straight pipes, obstacles and junctions, and lowered joint actuation effort throughout. It also allows the robot to adapt to changes in pipe diameter autonomously, without any change of command from the operator.

DSBC controller block diagram. Click to enlarge.

Arbitrary-angle junctions: generalized windowed rolling helix gait

The windowed rolling helix was originally formulated for perpendicular branches, with a fixed phase offset between the head and tail windows. I generalized that offset as a function of the branch angle, so a single formulation covers arbitrary junction geometries. The gait was validated on hardware at a 135° Y-junction.

Traversing a 135° Y-junction: (a)–(e) hardware over 36 seconds, (f) maneuver in PyBullet simulation. Click to enlarge.

Pipe geometry estimation: virtual chassis from proprioception

Beyond executing a maneuver, the robot needs to know what it is climbing. I developed a real-time estimation pipeline, built on the Virtual Chassis formalism, that recovers the pipe axis, the robot's orientation relative to it, and the pipe radius from joint encoders and IMUs alone. The standard SVD-based chassis-axis estimator degenerates at the wrap angles this gait employs, so I replaced it with a Levenberg–Marquardt centerline fit. Pipe inclination is estimated through a complementary filter fusing gyroscope and accelerometer measurements, achieving a mean error of approximately 2.5°.

I also corrected the radius model: the robot wraps the pipe as a polygon rather than a circle, so sampling radius at the joints over-reads the diameter, and measuring instead where the links contact the pipe reduces the error from +21% to under 4%.

Estimating pipe inclination and diameter from joint encoders and IMUs while the robot grips a tilted pipe.

Pipe profile reconstruction: gait-based odometry

Tracking displacement along the pipe from the gait's own cycles turns those per-instant estimates into a map. Each rolling-helix cycle advances the robot by a known amount along the pipe axis, so integrating over cycles gives distance travelled without any external reference. Combined with the estimated axis direction and radius at each point, this reconstructs the geometry the robot has passed through, including changes in inclination and diameter between segments.

Live pipe profile reconstruction from tilt and diameter estimations.

Junction detection: contact force from joint torque

Autonomous junction traversal requires the robot to detect a junction and determine its orientation relative to it. I am developing a model that estimates contact force from joint torque. Contact is decomposed into two components: a radial force, which reflects how tightly the robot is gripping the pipe, and an axial force along the pipe, which indicates the presence of a junction. Localizing where along the body that force acts identifies which modules are in contact with the junction — the information the windowed rolling helix needs to initiate a transition. The model is verified by applying known axial loads, with a real-time visualization showing the average gripping force alongside the applied external force and its location.

Known axial loads applied to the robot, with the estimated gripping force and the applied external force visualized in real time.

Research / 02

Caterpillar-inspired soft robot

Soft Robotics Lab, Bucknell University · advised by Prof. William Scott · 2022–2024

Locomotion and steering of the caterpillar-inspired soft robot.

Soft crawling robots can reach spaces rigid robots cannot, but their compliance makes them difficult to model and control. Most tendon-driven caterpillar robots advance by sliding their feet along the ground, which limits traction and makes steering impractical. Lifting the feet clear of the ground instead requires both a body whose segments can bend sufficiently in either direction and a gait that coordinates them. The objective of this project was to design a tendon-driven crawling robot capable of lifting its feet, together with the gaits that produce that motion, and to extend the design with steering.

Contributions

Hardware design

The robot is built from an alternating chain of flexible bending segments and rigid hubs, so it can be assembled with any number of segments. Each segment is 3D printed in FilaFlex, a thermoplastic elastomer, and acts as a spring: a tendon routed through guide tubes along its centerline is wound by a motor in the adjacent hub, bending the segment against its own elasticity. Prolegs printed in the same material support the body and provide friction at the ground.

The design difference that matters is the angle at which segments meet the hubs. Earlier tendon-driven caterpillar robots connect them flat, so the prolegs are parallel when the segments are straight. Connecting them at 45° places the neutral stance at the midpoint of each segment's range rather than at one end, leaving enough range of motion in both directions for neighbouring segments to coordinate and lift a foot clear of the ground.

Modular hardware: flexible tendon-driven segments alternating with rigid hubs. Click to enlarge.

Kinematic model and gait design

We modelled the robot as a planar quasi-static system in which each flexible segment bends into a circular arc, so its configuration reduces to a few shape variables and a gait becomes a periodic trajectory through that space. Designing gaits this way produced crawling that lifts the feet clear of the ground rather than relying on sliding, and displacement per cycle grows with both segment count and amplitude.

Model geometry: hub poses follow from the segment bending angles. Click to enlarge.

Simulated (left) and experimental (right) snapshots through one stance interval. Click to enlarge.

Steering

Steering required a second degree of freedom. I designed a modified segment with a fishbone structure — a central backbone with ribs on either side routing a second pair of tendons — and thickened the backbone so its bending stiffness matches the standard segment. Winding those tendons in opposite directions bends the segment out of plane in either direction.

With the steering segment placed at the center of a three-segment robot, turning rate is linear in the steering pulley angle up to a saturation point, beyond which the robot risks tipping. Placing it at the front or rear gave lower turning rates and less stability.

Overhead view: the steering angle Ψ is measured between the first and last hubs. Click to enlarge.

Research / 03

Centipede-inspired soft robot

Soft Robotics Lab, Bucknell University · advised by Prof. William Scott · 2023–2025

The centipede-inspired robot crawling on a bench.

Centipedes combine a travelling wave along the body with the lifting of successive leg pairs. Reproducing that in a robot typically requires separate actuators for body bending and leg motion, so the actuation and control burden grows with body length. The objective of this project was to realize that coupling mechanically, such that a single actuator per segment produces both body bending and leg lift.

Contributions

A segment that couples body bending and leg lift

Centipedes coordinate a wave along the body with the lifting of each pair of legs. I designed a modular segment that reproduces that coupling mechanically: one servo per segment drives a pair of flexible links, which bend the body and raise the legs together, so the coupled motion comes from the mechanism rather than from coordinating separate actuators.

Gait control

An offboard controller drives the segments through a traveling wave, producing the crawling gait shown above. The robot is built to be extended segment by segment, so gaits can be tested across different body lengths.

Research / 04

Sub-gram aerial robot flapping-wing simulation

Soft and Micro Robotics Lab, MIT · advised by Prof. Yufeng (Kevin) Chen · summer 2024

The wing sweeps through a stroke angle while pitching passively; the aerodynamic force on the chord varies through the stroke.

At insect scale, direct actuation of every wing degree of freedom is impractical. A dielectric elastomer actuator drives the stroke, while wing pitch arises passively from aerodynamic loading about a compliant hinge. Wing orientation, and consequently the lift generated, must therefore be predicted rather than prescribed. The objective of this project was to model the coupled stroke and pitch motion, and to predict the aerodynamic forces it produces over a stroke.

Contributions

Wing motion model

A flapping wing is driven at its root but twists passively under aerodynamic load, so its orientation at any instant is not commanded directly. I built a MATLAB model that predicts the wing's position through the stroke from the active stroke motion and the passive pitching that results, giving the state of any point on the wing at each timestep.

Simulated wing chord through one stroke, with the leading edge marked in red. Click to enlarge.

Aerodynamic force prediction

From that motion, the model predicts the aerodynamic forces the wing generates over a stroke — the quantity that sets how much lift the soft actuator driving the wing has to produce.
